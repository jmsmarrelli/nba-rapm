Method notes

The reconstruction rules in full, the estimator's choices, and where I think this is weakest.

Rebuilding a lineup

For each game:

  1. Roster. Both teams' available players come from the box score, not the log. This matters more than it sounds: a player who checks in, plays two minutes and never touches the ball appears in the log exactly twice, and both appearances are substitutions.

  2. Period-start five. Within each period, for every player, record the index of his first substitution in and the index of his first appearance in any other event, including being substituted out. He started the period if the second exists and comes first, or if it exists and he never entered.

Being substituted out counts as evidence of presence, which is what makes the rule work for a player who starts a period and does nothing before sitting down.

  1. Fallback. If period one does not resolve to five a side, the box score's starter flag is used. Periods two onward have no fallback.

  2. Walk. Apply substitutions in order. If a team's on-court set ever leaves five, the game is dropped.

  3. Check. Accumulate floor time per player from the clock, compare against box score minutes, and drop the game if the mean absolute error across players exceeds 1.5 minutes or any single player is off by more than 6.

Steps 4 and 5 are the ones that matter for trust. The check is not a formality — it is what catches a period whose starting five resolved to five wrong players, which the walk itself has no way to notice.

What the failures look like

Reason Meaning
lineup a period would not resolve to five a side, or a substitution broke the five
minutes_mean the whole game's floor time drifted from the box score
minutes_max one player's did
no_box / no_box_minutes the box score for that game is missing or empty

The 2017-18 through 2019-20 seasons carry far more lineup failures than the rest, which points at the source feed rather than at the rule: the same code run on 2021-22 keeps 98.9% of games.

Counting possessions

The usual estimator is FGA + 0.44×FTA − OREB + TOV. It is a good season-level approximation and a poor stint-level one, because the 0.44 is a league-average guess about which free throws end a trip and stints are three or four possessions long.

Since the log is available, possessions are counted instead. Every event that unambiguously identifies who has the ball is marked with that team — shots, free throws, rebounds (offensive to the shooting team, defensive to the other), turnovers — and the sequence of marks is compressed into runs. Each run is a possession.

Events that do not identify possession (fouls, timeouts, jump balls, substitutions) are skipped rather than guessed at. That is safe: dropping a marker can only merge two possessions that were already adjacent and identical, and adjacent identical marks collapse anyway.

The case this handles that the formula does not is the and-one. Made field goal and free throw are both marked to the same team, they are adjacent, and they collapse into the single possession they actually were.

The resulting count comes out at 96.3 possessions per team per game across the sample. Published pace figures for these seasons sit a shade higher, around 98 to 100, and the gap is the possessions that end without a marker the log identifies — a period expiring on a missed shot with no rebound recorded, most often. It is a level shift of two or three per cent that applies to every stint equally, so it scales every rating by the same small factor and does not move anyone's rank.

The design matrix

One row per stint-side. Ten ones: five players in the offense block, five in the defense block, plus a home indicator.

  • Outcome: 100 × points ÷ possessions for that stint-side.
  • Weight: possessions.
  • Defensive coefficients are reported sign-flipped, so positive is good on both sides.

Ridge, sparse_cg, intercept unpenalized. 188,329 rows by 1,563 columns for the three-season window, which solves in a couple of seconds.

Why folds are split by game

Stints inside a game share a defense, a venue, a night's shooting, and mostly the same twenty players. Randomly splitting rows lets the model score a stint using another stint from the same game, which flatters every model in the comparison and flatters the unregularised one most, because it is the one memorising.

On the penalty

The cross-validation curve is shallow between roughly 1,000 and 8,000 — the difference between the best and worst penalty in that range is under a tenth of a percent of MSE. That is worth being honest about: the exact choice of 4,000 barely matters, and the ranking of players is stable across the whole plateau. What matters enormously is being somewhere on the plateau rather than at either end.

Things I considered and did not do

A box-score prior. Every production metric starts players at a prior derived from their counting stats, then lets the plus-minus data pull them away. It improves the ratings. It also makes the comparison in run_04_boxscore.py circular, because the thing being compared to the box score would already contain it. Left out on purpose.

Recency weighting. A three-season window treats a possession from 2020 the same as one from 2023. Real systems decay it. Adding decay would help the forecast and cost the simplicity of "this window, these games, this fit".

Filtering garbage time. Every threshold is arbitrary, and possession weighting already makes blowout minutes cheap relative to close ones. I would revisit this if the ratings were going into a decision rather than a repository.

Bootstrapped intervals. Resampling games and refitting would give an honest error bar per player. It is a few hours of compute and the right next step, and its absence is the single biggest gap in what is here.

Where I think this is weakest

Collinearity is managed, not solved. Ridge stops the model from making wild claims about players it cannot separate. It does not let it separate them. Two players who share 95% of their minutes get similar ratings because the data genuinely cannot distinguish them, and the penalty makes that look like agreement rather than ignorance. Nothing in the output flags which players are in that position, and it should.

The three thin seasons. 2017-18 to 2019-20 keep 80-85% of games. If the games that fail are not random — and there is no reason to assume they are — then those seasons contribute a slightly biased sample, and the ten-season forecast exercise runs across them.

Ratings are league-relative. They are not value over replacement and should not be summed into a team total without care. The forecast in stage 4 does possession-weight them into a team prediction, which is a legitimate use, but a salary or trade valuation would need a replacement baseline this does not provide.

One number per player per window. No role adjustment, no lineup interactions, no split by opponent quality. A player whose value depends on fitting a specific scheme gets one number that averages over the schemes he played in.
